CHAPTER 01

The short version

If you read nothing else, these five facts explain almost every surprise the S8 has thrown at us.

  • It is two computers in one box. An Android 10 tablet does the screen, apps and media. A small separate chip, the MCU, is wired to the car: it watches the ignition, the headlights and the steering-wheel buttons, and tells Android what happened.

  • On top of Android sits the FYT layer. About fifty system apps (their names start with com.syu) run the radio, Bluetooth, cameras and settings. They also decide which apps keep running when you switch the car off.

  • Almost everything that defines the unit is read-only. Without root or reflashing you cannot edit system files. You can change settings, give apps special permissions, and feed a few files to the SD-card updater.

  • “Off” means asleep, and sleep kills your apps. About 30 seconds after the key goes off, FYT force-stops every app you installed. A force-stopped app is in the stopped state: it hears nothing from Android, not even “the unit has started”, until something launches it by name. The best rescue is ATOTO's keep-alive service (chapter 8).

  • Security is looser than on a phone. There is no root, but SELinux, Android's inner rulebook, is set to “permissive”: it writes down rule-breaking and lets it happen. That is why the brightness scheduler can read the real backlight level. A firmware update could tighten this, so apps must cope if it does.

CHAPTER 02

Two computers, many layers

Software on the S8 is stacked like floors in a building. Each floor relies on the one below and is managed by different people. The lower the floor, the more dangerous it is to touch, and the less of it you can reach.

The floors, bottom to top

FloorWhat it is, in plain words
HardwareA Unisoc UIS7862 processor (8 cores, a mix of fast and power-saving ones), 4 GB of memory, a 1280×800 screen that is physically mounted sideways and turned upright in software, an NXP radio tuner and a Realtek Bluetooth chip. There is no light sensor, so the unit cannot tell day from night by itself.
Radio firmwareSmall programs that run inside the cellular modem, Wi-Fi/Bluetooth and GPS chips, plus calibration data (“NV”). Damage here can't be undone at home.
Boot chainThe first code that runs at power-on. It checks and loads the next stage. Includes a tiny trusted OS that guards secrets, and a map of the hardware (dtbo).
KernelLinux, the core that talks to the hardware. Drivers live in a separate partition called socko. The boot partition also holds the recovery program that installs updates.
Android 10The standard Android operating system as Unisoc delivered it, plus a full set of Google apps (Play Store, Maps, YouTube, Chrome, Gboard).
FYT / SYU layerFYT is the Chinese software house behind many aftermarket head units (Joying, Teyes clones and others run the same base; the XDA community documents it in depth). Its apps turn a tablet into a car stereo. They live on their own partition, mounted at /oem.
ATOTO additionsA thin layer on top of FYT: a keep-alive service, a usage-tracking app, an eSIM app, a voice assistant, and seed apps that copy themselves into your apps on first start. ATOTO barely changes FYT's behaviour.
Your appsEverything you install, plus all settings and app data (/data). This is the only floor that changes while the unit runs.
Why the MCU matters

Many of the S8's habits come from the MCU, not from Android. When you switch the key off, the MCU tells Android “ignition off” and Android goes to sleep instead of shutting down. When you switch the headlights on, the MCU reports it and FYT dims the screen. Apps never talk to the MCU directly; they only see what FYT does in response.

CHAPTER 04

Protected areas: what you can change, and how

Picture the unit as rings of protection. The further in you go, the bigger the tool you need and the worse a mistake gets. Most useful work happens in the two outer rings.

1 · Your app, with permissions

Change screen brightness, draw overlays, run in the background, read GPS, talk to USB devices, opt in to the keep-alive. Details in chapter 8.

2 · adb shell from the Mac

Grant special permissions with no taps, change any Android setting, install and remove apps, read logs, take screenshots. No root needed.

3 · SD-card updater

Replace the boot logo and animation and FYT's two settings files. Careful: the stock package also wipes your data.

4 · Reflash an image

Needed to edit anything on /oem, such as the sleep whitelist, or to remove preinstalled apps. Possible in principle, untested, real brick risk.

5 · Never

Bootloader, trusted OS, modem, calibration, drivers, MCU firmware, and the screen-tuning files that crashed the kernel. Recovery needs factory tools and files we don't have.

Who holds the keys

Android gives every program an identity, and the identity decides what it may do. Three identities matter on the S8.

WhoIdentityWhat it can do
FYT services and ATOTO's keep-aliveThe “system” user (uid 1000)Force-stop or launch any app, set system properties, override brightness, ignore the sleep sweep. The building manager with the master keys.
adb shell (you, from the Mac)The “shell” userChange Android settings, grant special permissions, call some system services, install apps, read logs. Cannot write to read-only partitions or change another app's insides.
An app you installIts own sandboxOnly what its permissions allow. Can't see other apps' files.
RootNobodyNot available. This is a locked “user” build: ro.debuggable=0, signed with release keys, and adb asks for key approval.
Locked, but leaky

SELinux is a second set of rules inside Android that decides which program may touch which file. On a phone it blocks anything not allowed. On the S8 it is in permissive mode: think of a guard who writes every violation in a notebook and waves everyone through. Two consequences:

  • Some reads work that shouldn't, such as the real backlight level. Each one leaves an avc: denied line in the log, and those lines flood the log buffer.
  • The Android security patch is from February 2020. A future firmware might switch SELinux to “enforcing”. Apps that rely on a leak must treat a failed read as “unknown” and carry on.

CHAPTER 05

Turning the key: the daily cycle

The S8 almost never truly shuts down. Switching the car off puts it to sleep: the memory stays powered and the uptime clock keeps counting. Only a power loss or a reboot starts it from scratch. What happens around sleep is what makes or breaks a background app. Measured

The four behaviours behind the timeline

BehaviourWhat happens
The sleep sweepAbout 30 s after the key goes off, FYT's main service (com.syu.ms) force-stops every third-party app in one batch. Spared: the app on screen, and apps on FYT's read-only LaunchWhiteList (the factory TPMS app is on it by name; our tire-pressure app is not).
The “stopped” stateForce-stopping hangs a “do not disturb” sign on the app. Android then delivers nothing to it: no “boot completed”, no alarms, no broadcasts. Only an explicit launch by name takes the sign down. The force-stop also switches off the app's accessibility service.
Reopen last appAt key-off FYT remembers the app on screen (sTopAppWhenMcuOff) and launches it again about 2 s after wake. If the home screen was showing, there is nothing to reopen.
Custom Auto Start AppOne app picked in OEM Settings → General. FYT launches it by name on every wake and about 10 s after a full boot, which works even from the stopped state. Only one app fits, it must be set by hand, and reinstalling that app silently clears the choice.

A cold start runs through five stages. The first three are the same on any Android device; the last two are where FYT and ATOTO step in. From firmware Measured

After a real restart

The system bar already does this: it has a hidden battery-voltage readout behind the property persist.fyt.voltage_visible, which didn't appear when set to 1 in a quick test. It adds 4 to the raw value before scaling, a fixed 0.4 V correction. On this unit that reads low, and more so as the voltage rises, so the error is in the scale, not an offset: Measured

An app that shows the battery voltage should use about 0.107 V per step, or better, let the user calibrate it against a meter: two readings are a small sample, the value moves in 0.1 V steps, and the divider in front of the MCU may differ between units. FYT's formula would show a healthy 14.3 V charging voltage as 13.7 V.

The switchboard: system properties

Android has a list of named switches called system properties, such as ro.fyt.launcher=com.android.launcher6. FYT's apps read 569 of their own. Names tell you their nature:

  • ro.* is fixed at boot (read-only).
  • persist.* is saved and survives reboots. Custom Auto Start App is one: persist.sys.navi.packagename.
  • sys.* is live and resets at reboot. Some start services, for example sys.fyt.carplay=1 starts the CarPlay server.

Apps can read most of them. Setting them needs system rights, or the ToolkitService door, or a new config.txt through the SD-card updater. Untested keys that look interesting: persist.fyt.hidewifi, sys.lsec.sleep_when_acc_off, sys.lsec.status_bar_height, sys.fyt.splitscreen, persist.syu.backui3rd (third-party reverse-camera UI).

Hidden doors

  • Factory settings: code 3368 in the FYT settings app (also present: 8888, 126). The factory pages expose many of the properties above.
  • Factory test app: type 000.= in the Calculator.
  • Launcher swap: launcher6 and launcher8 are both installed; any third-party launcher also works.

CHAPTER 07

Worked example: the headlight brightness tug-of-war

This one story shows how FYT acts behind Android's back, and how an app copes. Android keeps a saved brightness setting from 1 to 255, and on the S8 it drives the backlight one-to-one. But whenever the headlights change, FYT puts a temporary brightness on top (26 with headlights on, 1 with them off). The saved setting doesn't change and nothing announces the change, so an app that only watches the setting never notices. Measured

The lesson for any app

On this unit, don't trust the saved setting to describe reality. Check the real state, react to differences, and back off if a fix doesn't stick. The real backlight reads 0 while the screen is off; that isn't an override. Reading it at all depends on SELinux being permissive, which is why the scheduler treats an unreadable file as “unknown”.

Measured

Watching the setting has a trap too. A ContentObserver tells an app that a setting changed, but it reads the current value when it fires. Several quick writes produce several callbacks that all read the last value. To tell your own writes from the user's, match them by value within a short time window, and don't "use up" a match on the first callback.

Reverse gear: the only signal is FYT's

The obvious places stay still when the car goes into reverse: the property sys.car.reverse, sys.fyt.mcu_reverse and the files in /sys/lsecdcamctl don't change. The signal exists, but only inside FYT's main service. Measured

  1. The reversing-light wire on the S8's harness goes live. A chip on the camera input (I2C driver sensor_sub) sees it, and the kernel reports a car-reverse hardware event.
  2. com.syu.ms publishes update 12 (U_BACKCAR, 1 = reverse, 0 = out of reverse) on its main module, within 45 ms.
  3. The reverse camera app, which subscribes to that update, opens. Back in park, the update goes to 0 and the camera closes.

The MCU reports reverse too, but it doesn't open the camera. It sends 01 00 23 03 every 100 ms while the car is in reverse and 01 00 23 02 for about a second after, arriving about 50 to 80 ms before the hardware event. com.syu.ms uses it only to turn the audio down (volume 4 to 2 on this car, restored on leaving reverse). Two kinds of overlay

The brightness scheduler dims the screen past the backlight's lowest step with a translucent amber layer. Android offers two ways to draw such a layer, and on the S8 they behave differently. Use the switch to compare them.

Surviving the sleep sweep

Every background app on the S8 has to answer one question: after FYT force-stops me, who launches me again? There are three answers, and only one needs no setup from the user.

How route A works

ATOTO's keep-alive service is itself protected from the sweep. Every 3 seconds it looks for apps that declare a service with the action com.atoto.keepalive and aren't running, and starts that service by name. A start by name works even on a stopped app. The app's side is a few lines in its manifest:

<service android:name=".KeepAliveService" android:exported="true">
  <intent-filter>
    <action android:name="com.atoto.keepalive" />
  </intent-filter>
</service>

Two catches, both found the hard way:

  • The service must put up its notification (startForeground) within 5 seconds or Android kills the app.
  • Unisoc's PowerController quietly blocks one app starting another unless the target allows “launched by another app”. The app sets that for itself through the power_ex service (setting type 5 to value 2), using a small launch-permission helper that each example app carries its own copy of. Never call power_ex code 31: it crashed the whole Android system once.

To opt out, the app disables its own keep-alive service; the keep-alive skips disabled ones. That is what the “Keep alive” switch in both example apps does.

What the app does when it comes back

  • Do the recovery in Application.onCreate, which runs however the process was started: start the foreground service, re-enable the accessibility service, re-apply its state.
  • FYT's launches (reopen-last-app and Custom Auto Start) open the app's main screen. To stay in the background, check who launched it: the system user and not the home screen means “auto-start”, so close the screen at once. A tap on the icon still opens it, because the launcher counts as a home app.
  • Re-check on every event that reaches a live process rather than trusting one alarm.

CHAPTER 10

Connecting from the Mac

adb (Android Debug Bridge) is the cable-free remote control we use for everything: installing builds, granting permissions, reading logs, taking screenshots. The S8's USB sockets only act as hosts (for drives and dongles), so USB debugging never appears. Wi-Fi works instead, after a one-time bootstrap.

  • Find the unit with adb mdns services; the address can change with the network, so read it from the output each time.
  • “offline” while the car is off is normal: the sleep sweep stops the Wi-Fi ADB app too. The unit's log keeps recording; read it after the key is back on.
  • After a wake or reboot, retry adb connect every 10 s; it takes 30 to 60 s.
  • Read what the system thinks with dumpsys: for example dumpsys display shows whether brightness is manual or FYT's temporary override, and dumpsys package shows whether an app is stopped.
  • The log buffer is small and fills with SELinux lines. Enlarge it first (adb logcat -G 4M) after every reboot.
Ask first

It's the car's unit. Force-stopping an app, rebooting, uninstalling or clearing app data can leave things dead until the next key cycle, so we always confirm before doing any of them.

CHAPTER 12

Worked example: the steering-wheel buttons

The car's steering-wheel remote is wireless. Its buttons talk by radio to a small receiver box behind the dash, and the box plugs into the steering-wheel control (SWC) wires on the back of the S8. Android never sees the radio, a USB device or a Bluetooth device. All the S8 sees is a resistance on a wire, and the MCU, not Android, decides which button that was. Measured

What the receiver box does

Each button connects a different resistance between the KEY wire and ground, which is a resistor ladder. The MCU feeds the wire a small voltage and measures what comes back with an 8-bit converter: 255 (FF) means nothing is pressed, and the lower the number, the lower the resistance. Opening the stock learning screen makes the MCU stream six raw readings every 100 ms. With all seven controls pressed one at a time on 2026-10-09, readings 2, 4 and 6 moved together and readings 1, 3 and 5 never left FF, so the receiver uses one wire. Measured

Measured

Why one notch can jump three steps. The receiver holds a resistance for about 0.3 to 0.9 s per tap. The MCU sends the first volume step at once, waits 0.5 s, then repeats every 0.1 s while the wire stays low. A single notch on 2026-10-09 gave three steps (at 0, 0.50 and 0.60 s), and quick flicks merge into one long press. Android applies each step within about 50 ms, so the lag and overshoot come from the MCU and the receiver, not from Android.

Learning: where the mapping lives

The stock Steering Wheel app shows a grid of 24 functions; learned ones are filled in. To teach a button: open the app, hold the wheel button until the app asks you to pick, tap the function, repeat for the other buttons, then Save and Exit. A short tap only shows readings; a hold starts learning. The screen closes by itself after about a minute. Measured

  • The learned table lives in the MCU, so it survives Android updates and data wipes. On this unit seven functions were learned: volume up and down, previous, next, play/pause, rear camera and front camera. None of the phone functions were. Measured
  • The function behind each custom key (cameras, voice, EQ…) is saved in com.syu.ms's private settings file (“Steer”), which an app can't read. FYT can also load a key list from steer_config.properties on the SD card or /oem; neither file exists on this unit. From firmware
  • Fixing the phone and skip buttons means re-learning them in the stock app. Nothing needs changing on the Android side.
Watch it yourself

FYT has a hidden switch that logs every MCU message: adb shell setprop persist.lsec.debug_mcu true, then adb logcat -s MCU. The shell may set it because SELinux is permissive. It also logs the MCU's status report every second (chapter 5), so switch it off afterwards with setprop persist.lsec.debug_mcu "".

For app builders

An app sits downstream of the MCU. It can't see a press the MCU drops, and it can't fix debounce or merged clicks. The steering data com.syu.ms publishes through ToolkitService (module 10) only flows while the learning screen is open. In normal use an app sees only the result: a volume change or a media key delivered to whatever is playing. To get every press with no MCU in between, use a Bluetooth or USB remote that acts as a keyboard (HID): Android reads those directly.

Still unknown

  • Open question Does the updater check the signature of a modified system image? Community reports say FYT updaters don't; not verified here.
  • Open question What format is AllAppUpdate.bin? Knowing it would allow app-level changes to /oem without a full flash.
  • Open question Does the keep-alive sometimes revive apps before the unit falls asleep? Seen once with the brightness scheduler (0.17 s after the force-stop); three runs of the tire-pressure app came back only after wake.
  • Open question What wrote an unexpected brightness value (253) while the OEM settings app was open?
  • Open question Why does the MCU report six steering-wheel readings per sample, with three of them moving for every button? Two KEY wires measured three ways is a guess; the three readings don't scale like one resistor against three pull-ups.
  • Open question What do the values in the MCU's learned table mean? Learned buttons hold small numbers (1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 24), and the same number appears twice, so they aren't raw readings or unique button IDs.
  • Open question What should the camera custom keys do? Their messages arrived but nothing visible happened.
  • Open question Is the battery-voltage scale (about 0.107 V per step instead of FYT's 0.1 V plus 0.4 V) the same on every S8, or does each unit need calibrating? Measured on one unit at two points.
  • Open question What unit is the MCU's board-current value in (121 with the key on and the engine presumably off)? No app on the unit displays it.
